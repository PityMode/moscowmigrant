    Bound by the Rules: War's Morality and the Just Defender

    Being the wronged side in a war does not release you from its moral rules — it is exactly what binds you to them. The just defender may strike military targets without limit, but may not make war on civilians, and that discipline is what keeps the just side just.

    There is a comfortable assumption that takes hold the moment you are persuaded which side in a war is in the right. It runs like this: the aggressor has forfeited every claim on our conscience, and therefore the side defending itself has been handed a kind of moral blank check. Whatever the wronged side does, it does in self-defense; whatever it suffers, it suffers innocently; and so the rules of war apply to the criminal alone and not to his victim. This is a seductive idea, and it is wrong. Being the just side in a war does not exempt you from its moral rules. It is precisely what obliges you to keep them.

    Two opposite errors crowd in here, and both corrupt judgment. The first is the blank-check error just described — the belief that righteousness is a license. The second is its mirror image, the lazy shrug that says “war is hell, both sides commit atrocities, so there is nothing to choose between them.” These two look like enemies, but they feed each other: the blank check provokes the shrug, and the shrug excuses the blank check. The way out of both runs through a single, unglamorous discipline: the laws of war bind everyone, and the just defender most of all, because keeping them is the only thing that lets him stay just.

    The defender is still bound by the rules#

    Start with the hard case, the one that tests the principle rather than flattering it. Suppose a country is fighting a genuine war of national liberation — attacked first, fighting for its survival, unambiguously the wronged party. Does that status dissolve the laws of war for its soldiers? It does not. Being the aggressor’s victim gives the defender no unlimited license: an army waging an entirely just war can still commit war crimes, and when it does, they are crimes — not regrettable necessities, not the aggressor’s fault by transitive property, but crimes.

    History furnishes the example that breaks the blank check most cleanly. Hitler attacked the Soviet Union first; nobody disputes the aggression or its monstrousness. And yet that prior aggression does not absolve the mass rapes committed by Soviet troops as they entered Königsberg. The Red Army was, in the largest sense, on the right side of that war; its soldiers were nonetheless capable of atrocity, and the atrocity remained an atrocity. To say that because Hitler started it nothing the avenging army did could count as a crime is to abandon morality at the very moment you most loudly claim it.

    So the principle holds even at its hardest. But this raises the only question that does real work: if a defending army can both fight justly and commit crimes, what separates the legitimate act of war from the war crime? The answer is intent. The line does not run between violence and restraint — war is violence — but between violence aimed at the enemy’s capacity to fight and violence aimed deliberately at civilians. A strike that destroys a military objective is an act of war however much it costs; a strike whose purpose is to kill civilians is a crime however it is dressed up. Everything turns on what was aimed at, and why.

    When the arithmetic applies#

    Here a familiar moral puzzle is worth confronting, because it is usually invoked to forbid exactly the choices war forces on the defender. In peacetime we hold to a strict prohibition: you may not deliberately sacrifice one innocent life to save several. The runaway trolley, the doctor who will not carve up one patient to save five — the moral intuition recoils, rightly, from treating a person as raw material for someone else’s salvation. That prohibition is real, and in ordinary life it should stand.

    But war is not ordinary life, and the arithmetic forbidden in peace becomes legitimate in a defensive war. When a country is fighting for its existence, a strike that knowingly costs an innocent life can be justified — on one absolute condition: that no alternative existed. The hard cases are those where an innocent person is used, without their knowledge, to accomplish a strike on a legitimate target, and the test there admits no rhetorical escape: was there another way to do it? If there was none, the killing falls inside the laws of war; if the objective could have been reached another way and that way was discarded for convenience or cruelty, the innocent death was a war crime. That condition is what keeps “the arithmetic applies in war” from sliding into “anything goes in war.” It is not a loophole but its opposite: it obliges the just side to look for the cleaner path first, and convicts it of a crime when it does not. The defender who internalizes this is not weakened by it. He is disciplined by it, and the discipline is the whole point.

    Conscription is not a crime, and neither is killing a general#

    Before going further it is worth clearing away two charges propaganda loves to file against the defender, because both are confusions about what the laws of war actually forbid.

    The first is the accusation that conscription itself is a kind of crime — that a state which compels its citizens to fight has already “become the dragon” it resists. This collapses on contact with the meaning of the word. Mobilization is coercive by definition; coercion is what mobilization is. Every state that borders a hostile neighbor is obliged to maintain a conscript army, because a purely volunteer force — the three hundred Spartans — is always too small for real defense. Whether that necessary coercion degrades into genuine abuse, into barrier troops and the torture of recruits, is a question of a state’s governance, not of the principle of conscription. The principle is clean; its perversions are a separate matter, and they are not entailed by it.

    The second confusion is graver and more useful to the aggressor: the claim that killing an enemy commander, or destroying a weapons depot, is “terrorism” rather than war. It is not. A man who has committed war crimes makes himself a lawful target everywhere — at the front, in the rear, at his desk, at his home. When General Igor Kirillov, the head of Russia’s chemical-defense forces, was killed by a bomb in December 2024 on Moscow’s Ryazansky Prospekt, that was a legitimate act of war, not an act of terror. Kirillov had ordered the use of banned chemical weapons and pushed the lie about American biolabs in Ukraine; he was a combatant and a war criminal, and his location deep in the rear changed nothing about his status. To strike a general is to strike the enemy’s military capacity in its most concentrated form. The defender who reaches such a target is not crossing a moral line; he is doing the most defensible thing an army at war can do — attacking the means of waging the war, rather than the enemy’s civilians.

    “Both sides do it” — and why that settles nothing#

    Now turn to the second error, the shrug. It will be objected that all of this is naive, because every army commits war crimes — and in the narrow factual sense the objection is correct. Every army in history has committed war crimes: Ukraine’s, America’s, every army that has ever existed. Soldiers under fire, frightened, enraged, exhausted, will sometimes do terrible things, and no flag has ever been clean of this. Anyone who claims his side is the single exception in the history of warfare is lying or dreaming.

    But notice what this concession does and does not establish. It establishes that war crimes occur on every side. It does not establish that the sides are therefore equal — and the leap from the first to the second is the central swindle of the false-symmetry view. The decisive question is not whether atrocities occur, because they always do; it is what the chain of command does about them. And on that question armies differ absolutely. In one army, atrocity is an aberration: when it happens, command suppresses it, prosecutes it, treats it as a stain to be punished. In another, atrocity is policy — ordered from above, broadcast as a warning, rewarded as zeal. These are not two points on a single scale; they are two different relationships between a state and its own crimes. Bucha and Mariupol were not the failures of Russian command — they were its expression. The contrast is not that one side’s soldiers are angels and the other’s devils; soldiers are soldiers. It is that one side’s leadership represses what its soldiers do wrong while the other side’s leadership incites it. That difference is the whole moral universe, and the shrug that says “both sides commit war crimes” is engineered to make you stop thinking one sentence too early — before you reach the only sentence that matters.

    The shrug is often fed, too, by sheer fabrication. The lurid atrocity tales that aggressor propaganda manufactures — the castrations, the grenades, the cinematic horrors invented to muddy the comparison — exist precisely to flood the ledger with invented crimes so that the real and the fictional blur and the audience throws up its hands. Refusing to distinguish what was done from what was merely alleged is not even-handedness. It is the propagandist’s victory.

    Occupation is the test that cannot be faked#

    If you want to know which army stands in which relation to its own crimes, do not listen to the speeches; watch how each behaves toward the people who fall under its control. Occupation is the test that cannot be faked, because an occupying force is alone with a defenseless population and answerable, in the moment, to no one but its own command — so what it does there reveals what that command wants.

    The contrast is stark and concrete. In Russian-occupied parts of Ukraine, abuse of civilians has been routine. When Ukrainian forces, by contrast, held territory in Russia’s Kursk Oblast, there were no reported abuses of the civilian population. That is not an accident of temperament. It rests on machinery: a military prosecutor’s office created specifically to enforce the laws of war, explicit instructions on discipline, the deliberate supply of food and water to civilians under control. Two things drive the restraint. One is the quality and training of the troops. The other is a conscious refusal to hand the enemy a propaganda gift — a recognition, at the level of command, that an atrocity is not only a moral catastrophe but a strategic one. An army that thinks this way about occupied civilians is one whose command does not want atrocities; an army that produces Bucha is one whose command does.

    The salvo that aims at no soldier#

    The clearest proof of intent is what a military chooses to hit when it has the whole map to choose from. A massed missile barrage costing hundreds of millions of dollars is not the act of an enraged conscript; it is a deliberate decision taken at the top, with targets selected in advance — so the targets indict the chooser. When wave after wave of missiles falls on a city and the only “military” object hit is a factory making military clothing — worth less than the single missile spent on it — while strikes costing over a billion dollars, and later hundreds of millions more, produce a near-zero military result, the conclusion is not that the aiming was poor. It is that the civilians were the aim. A campaign that spends fortunes to destroy apartment blocks and almost nothing of military value is not a campaign against an enemy’s defensive capacity. It is a war of extermination against a people, conducted by other means — and the military target the defender’s arithmetic requires was never the point.

    Genocide is defined by intent, not by the count#

    This brings us to the most abused word in the vocabulary of war, and to a distinction propaganda works tirelessly to erase. Genocide is not a measure of how many died. It is a measure of what the killing was for: what makes an act genocide is the aim of destroying a group as such, and that aim, not the body count, is what the word names.

    The consequence is uncomfortable for both errors at once. A smaller number of deaths can constitute genocide if the intent is to erase a people, while a larger number need not, if the intent was to defeat an army. This is why aggressor and defender are not symmetrical even when both kill, and even when the defender kills more. The question is always: what was being attempted? When a force attacks with the sole purpose of killing the members of a group because of who they are — as in the assault whose only goal was the killing of Jews — that is genocidal in intent, whatever its scale. When a force responds to destroy the military that committed the attack, that is defense, whatever its scale. To collapse the two because the second produced more casualties is to substitute arithmetic for the very thing the word genocide exists to capture: purpose.

    It follows that the accusation of genocide is itself a weapon, and a precious one to the side actually doing the exterminating. When a genocide charge is leveled against a state whose campaign aims at an armed force rather than at a people, it does not describe a reality; it inverts one — taking the word forged to name the deliberate destruction of a people and turning it into a slur against the people defending themselves from exactly that. The honest test never changes: not how many, but why.

    Every crime has a co-author — which is not the same as an excuse#

    I have insisted that the just defender’s crimes are real crimes, and I hold to that. But one more distinction must be made, carefully, because it is easy to abuse in either direction. When an army fighting a just war does commit a crime — when a strike that should not have happened kills civilians, including children, in an enemy city — that crime has a co-author. The aggressor who created the war, who manufactured the situation in which such a strike became possible, bears authorship of it too, in the same way the dictator who launched a war of conquest is co-author of every atrocity his victims’ avenging armies later commit on his soil.

    This is not an erasure of the defender’s responsibility — and everything depends on the emphasis. The co-author does not absolve the author: the soldier who commits the crime is still guilty of it, and the just side’s command must still suppress and prosecute it. What co-authorship establishes is narrower and truer — that moral judgment must trace the causal chain back to its origin, to the one who is the source of the war’s evil. To remember the co-author is not to excuse the immediate perpetrator; it is to refuse the aggressor the satisfaction of pointing at his victim’s worst moment and crying “you see — you are no better than I am.” He is the reason the moment exists. That is the difference between explaining a crime and pardoning it, and the just side must do the first without ever doing the second.

    This matters beyond the ledger of guilt. Crimes filmed and broadcast by their perpetrators do a particular lasting damage — and not, in the end, to their victims. The soldier who publishes himself executing at point-blank range a prisoner who has just said “Slava Ukraini” is not destroying his enemy’s future; that enemy will recover. He is destroying his own country’s future, foreclosing for years any normal relations with the world, because such images are never forgotten. The aggressor’s broadcast atrocities are a confession that outlives the war — the most durable evidence of which command wanted what.

    The slide toward false symmetry#

    It remains to name the intellectual failure that makes all of this harder to see, because it is committed not by propagandists but by people who consider themselves sophisticated. There is a steady degradation in liberal commentary, a step-by-step slide toward treating aggressor and victim as two instances of the same thing. It dresses itself in the language of nuance — “both sides’ narratives,” competing myths, negotiating positions that mirror one another — and it treats the defending nation as having no will of its own, a mere function of its backers, so that the question of who is right quietly disappears into the question of which great power will prevail. The most revered commentators are not immune; some have been among the first to slide. This is the educated form of the shrug, more dangerous than the crude form precisely because it sounds like wisdom. To frame the aggressor’s demands and the defender’s survival as equivalent “fetishes,” to treat the wish to not be conquered as just another bargaining posture, is to have lost the thread that connects facts to judgment. False symmetry is not neutrality. It is a decision, made under cover of detachment, to stop distinguishing the one who set the fire from the ones trying not to burn.

    And so the two errors meet again at the end, as they did at the beginning. The blank check says the just side may do anything; the false symmetry says the just side is no better than the other; and both, in their opposite ways, destroy the only thing that makes a side actually just — its conduct. The just defender wins the moral argument by one means only: by being, in fact, more moral. He may strike the enemy’s army without limit and apologize to no one for it. He may not make war on the enemy’s civilians, and the day he does, he begins to forfeit the very thing he is fighting for. The discipline of that restraint is not a handicap imposed on the righteous by squeamish lawyers. It is the substance of righteousness itself — and the side that keeps the rules loses nothing real by fighting clean, while keeping the one thing no defeat can take and no victory won the other way can restore.
