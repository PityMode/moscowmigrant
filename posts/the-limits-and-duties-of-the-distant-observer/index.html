    The Limits and Duties of the Distant Observer

    Living abroad does not disqualify a person from judging foreign affairs, and the demand that an émigré keep silent is censorship dressed up as humility. But the freedom to analyze from a distance carries duties: deferring where competence ends, taking a side without lying, diagnosing rather than commanding, and answering for the effect that analysis has on the world it describes.

    There is a peculiar instruction that observers of foreign affairs hear constantly, and it always arrives in the same imperative tone. Do not interfere in American affairs. Do not interfere in European affairs. And if you no longer live in the country you came from, do not interfere in its affairs either. The logic, such as it is, runs like this: a person who watches a country from a distance has forfeited the right to an opinion about it, and any judgment he offers is an impertinence at best and a hostile intrusion at worst. The demand deserves to be taken seriously, because beneath its surface lies a genuine question — about who is entitled to speak, and about what honesty requires of anyone who does. The answer comes in two parts that may at first seem to pull against each other. The first is that the demand for silence is not modesty but censorship. The second is that the freedom to speak does not license one to say anything at all; it comes attached to duties that are easy to evade and important to honor.

    Distance Does Not Disqualify#

    Begin with the claim that location is decisive — that the person who has lived his whole life in a country necessarily understands it better than the one who left, or who is merely looking in from outside. This sounds like common sense, and it is false. An observer could, of course, restrict himself to writing only about the four walls of the room he sits in. He can see those walls perfectly well; no one could accuse him of overreach. But that would not be very interesting, and more to the point, it rests on a confusion. Proximity is not the same as understanding. Plenty of self-styled experts grasp almost nothing about their own countries, while a person armed with scholarship, with statistics, with the sober reading of crime data and sociology and the competing assessments of serious analysts, can arrive at a genuine judgment from thousands of kilometers away. What gives a person the right to judge is not a passport or a postal code. It is the capacity to analyze — to read the articles, to weigh the figures, to listen to the people who know more, and to think.

    To judge in this way is not to interfere in anyone’s internal affairs. It is to assess them. The difference matters: the analyst does not presume to govern, to vote in elections that are not his, or to set another country’s policy. He expresses an assessment. And expressing an assessment of any country’s conduct is not a transgression. It is the most ordinary exercise of free speech imaginable, no different in kind from a citizen of one country having opinions about the wars and crimes and follies of another. To forbid it — to insist that a person fall silent because of where he happens to sleep — is censorship, plain and simple. The refusal to obey that instruction is principled rather than merely stubborn. A person may go on judging American affairs, European affairs, the affairs of the country he left, and, if it should come to it, Australian affairs too, without owing anyone an apology for the address on his letters.

    Taking a Side Without Going Blind#

    Here a sharper objection arrives, and it deserves a real answer rather than a brush-off. If an observer plainly supports one side in a war, has he not surrendered the very objectivity that would make his judgment worth anything? Is he an analyst, the objection runs, or merely a partisan with a microphone?

    The premise — that these are mutually exclusive — should be rejected. No one performs a lobotomy on a person to remove the part of the brain responsible for analysis the moment he takes a political position. A human being is perfectly capable of holding a conviction and still describing reality as it is. It takes effort; it is not automatic; but it is entirely possible. One can support a side in a war without the slightest embarrassment and still refuse to pretend that its forces are advancing where they are in fact withdrawing, or that a retreat that plainly happened did not happen. Sympathy for one side does not oblige a person to lie for it. The propagandist is not someone who has chosen a side; the propagandist is someone who has agreed to falsify on its behalf. Those are different acts. One can stand firmly with the victim of an aggression and still refuse, absolutely refuse, to convert that sympathy into untruth.

    And the division between two sides in a war is not some artificial construct imposed on an innocent public to herd it into camps. It is the most natural division there could be. There is a war. One country attacked another. To support the aggressor or to support the victim is simply to face that fact and respond to it. No outside force imposes this choice; a person makes it by looking at what is in front of him. Those who complain that the world has been cruelly split into “for” and “against” are really complaining that a war is being fought, and a war does not leave much room for a comfortable third position above it all.

    This is not to claim a perch of total impartiality. An observer who chooses, in a given moment, to concentrate his critical attention on the conduct of the aggressor rather than on the internal failings of the side under attack is making a deliberate choice, not pronouncing a verdict that those failings do not exist. The two are different. To say “the central problem demanding analysis right now lies here, not there” is a judgment of proportion, and proportion is part of honest analysis. It does not require pretending to be the impartial register of all things — only being clear about which question the moment actually presses.

    Speaking Truth Without Joining Bad Company#

    A related discipline follows, and it is the most delicate of all: how to state an unwelcome truth without thereby allying oneself with the bad actors who say something that sounds the same. Shared rhetoric is not shared position. A criticism can be correct and still coincide, word for word, with a slogan that hostile propaganda has been repeating for its own ends. The temptation, faced with that overlap, is to fall silent — to suppress a true observation merely because an enemy has stumbled onto the same sentence. But silence purchased at the price of truth is its own kind of dishonesty.

    What distinguishes the honest critic from the propagandist whose words he happens to echo is not the sentence itself but the ground he stands on: his record, his evident purpose, the principle from which the criticism issues. A patriot who criticizes his own leadership from love of his country is not made an enemy by the accident that the country’s enemies criticize the same leadership from hatred. The two coincide on the surface and diverge entirely beneath it. The responsible analyst keeps that distinction in view, both for himself and for others. He does not refuse to say something true because the wrong people are also saying it; nor does he pretend that saying it has enlisted him in their cause. He states the truth and lets his grounds for stating it speak for themselves.

    Knowing Where Competence Ends#

    If honesty forbids lying for the side one favors, it equally forbids pretending to knowledge one does not have. This is the other face of the same coin, and it is the part that the loudest demanders of opinions tend to forget. People sometimes grow impatient with the habit of deferring to experts — why, they ask, must we always defer to specialists, whose vision supposedly gets blurred inside their closed professional worlds? Why can’t an ordinary person with a clear head simply pronounce on how to destroy a bridge, or how to resolve a humanitarian catastrophe, or what weapon will turn a war?

    The honest answer is often: because the analyst does not know. When the conversation turns to military affairs, to engineering, to the technical question of whether a particular munition can substitute for artillery, an analyst who has not studied these things is not equipped to reason about them, and he should refuse to perform a competence he lacks. What a person can say with certainty is what falls outside his own expertise — and on those matters the right course is to wait for the people who have actually studied them. This is not false modesty and it is not evasion. It is the precondition of being trustworthy on anything. A person who claims to know everything is reliable on nothing. So the analyst defers to the military expert and the engineer not because expertise is sacred, but because honest analysis requires an honest accounting of one’s own limits, and it is better to say “I don’t know, let me ask someone who does” than to fill the air with confident noise.

    The Analyst Diagnoses; He Does Not Prescribe#

    Competence has a second edge, and it concerns not only what the analyst knows but what kind of statement he is entitled to make. There is a difference between describing what is and commanding what should be — between diagnosis and prescription — and the distinction is easy to blur but essential to keep.

    Consider the difference between a physician and a campaigner. A doctor who diagnoses a terminal illness is not advocating for the patient’s death; he is naming a reality. To forecast that a state may fracture, or that a course of events is likely to end badly, is the work of an analyst, not an endorsement of that outcome. The analyst’s task is to describe what is — the situation as it actually stands, the trajectory the facts support. That is a different vocation from the politician’s, whose business is to bend reality toward a desired future. The campaigner is permitted, even obliged, to promote the outcome he wants. The analyst is not, because his authority rests entirely on his fidelity to what is, and the moment he starts selling a future he finds attractive he has stopped analyzing and started advocating.

    This imposes a hard discipline. Honesty forbids the analyst from dressing up a hopeful scenario he does not actually believe in, merely because his audience would like to hear it. If he sees no realistic path to some desired transformation, he may not pretend the path exists in order to comfort. To do so would be to deceive the very people who trust him to tell them the truth. The analyst diagnoses; he does not write the prescription, and he certainly does not forge a flattering one.

    The Observer Effect and Its Responsibilities#

    And yet the line between describing the world and acting on it is not as clean as the word “observer” suggests. There is a principle, borrowed from physics, that the act of observing alters the thing observed. Something analogous holds in public life. Commentary does not float above events like a neutral mirror; it enters the world it describes and changes it. A forecast can dishearten or embolden. An analysis, widely heard, can shift the mood of the very people whose actions it claims merely to predict. History is made by people, and the moods of those people are exactly what public speech touches. The observer is therefore never wholly outside the experiment.

    This places the responsible analyst on a scale, somewhere between two impossible extremes. At one end stands the fantasy of the perfectly detached cosmic observer, who reports on human affairs as if from another planet, untouched by and untouching the events below — a position no one who is actually heard can occupy. At the other end stands the propagandist, who has abandoned description altogether and speaks only to produce an effect, truth be damned. The honest analyst lives in the middle: aware that his words have consequences, and refusing to let that awareness license him to lie. He does not pretend his speech is inert, and he does not exploit its power to deceive.

    What this awareness demands, concretely, is responsibility for what one amplifies. If analysis shapes the morale of those who hear it, then feeding hopelessness is not a neutral act, and neither is inflating false hope. The analyst owes his audience an accurate picture — but accuracy includes the recognition that despair and delusion are themselves forces in the world, and that a careless forecast can become a small cause of the outcome it predicts. To speak responsibly is to weigh not only whether a thing is true but what it will do once said, and to refuse both the lie that comforts and the lie that demoralizes.

    The Duty Owed to the Audience#

    All of this converges on a final obligation, the one that gives the rest its point: the duty the distant observer owes to those he addresses. An analyst who works from afar does not thereby float free of any audience; on the contrary, he remains bound to the people he speaks to, and much of that audience may still live inside the very country he is judging. He cannot credibly shed his own origins and reinvent himself as a native of wherever he has landed. He remains who he is, addressing the people he came from, and that relationship carries a responsibility he cannot put down.

    The core of that responsibility is to support, not to anathematize, the ordinary people on the other end. There is a kind of commentary that curdles into contempt for one’s own audience — that addresses those who stayed behind as if they were a mass to be despised, written off, wished away. That is not analysis; it is a sickness, a self-hatred turned outward against the very people the work is meant to serve. The distant observer’s obligation runs the other way. He may judge governments without mercy and name crimes without flinching, while still treating the ordinary human beings he speaks to as people worth addressing honestly and addressing with care. The target of criticism is conduct, policy, power — not the audience itself.

    This, finally, is why the two halves of the answer belong together. The right to judge from a distance and the duty to judge honestly are not in tension; they are the same commitment seen from two sides. The freedom to speak about any country, regardless of where one lives, is a basic liberty that no one may revoke by ordering a person to be quiet. And precisely because it is a freedom and not a license, it carries obligations: to take a side without surrendering one’s eyes, to defer where one is ignorant, to diagnose rather than command, to answer for the effect one’s words have in the world, and to keep faith with the people one addresses. The distant observer who honors those duties is not hiding in a comfortable corner above the fray. He is doing something necessary and exacting, and he is doing it as honestly as he can. That, and not silence, is what the moment asks of him.
